\title{Exact Global Optimality for Packing Ten Unequal Circles}
\author{The unequal-circle proof project}
\date{October 9, 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We consider ten pairwise non-overlapping circles with radii
$r_i=\sqrt{i}$, $1\le i\le 10$, inside a circular container.  We give a
computer-assisted proof that the minimum container radius is an exact value
$\Rcrit$, defined as the unique solution of two explicit angular equations in
a rational rectangle.  A matching ten-circle realization exists at this
value, and a finite global certificate excludes every smaller radius.  The
lower-bound proof combines a complete radial and angular case tree, a
two-inequality angle barrier for the main seven-circle order, and
negative-cycle certificates for the four exceptional cases.  For every
hypothetical packing with $R<\Rcrit$, the composition accounts for all
$14{,}757{,}888$ sector assignments; the radial tree itself covers the larger
domain $R\le U$.  The proof decisions in the replay use integer and
rational arithmetic; the result is a reproducible computer-assisted proof,
not an end-to-end theorem-prover formalization.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Let $r_i=\sqrt{i}$ for $1\le i\le10$.  The problem is to find the smallest
radius $R$ for which there are centers $p_1,\ldots,p_{10}\in\mathbb R^2$
satisfying
\begin{align}
  \norm{p_i}+r_i&\le R
       &&(1\le i\le10), \label{eq:containment}\\
  \norm{p_i-p_j}&\ge r_i+r_j
       &&(1\le i<j\le10). \label{eq:separation}
\end{align}
Write $\Ropt$ for this minimum.  The minimum is attained: after fixing a
bounded interval of candidate radii, all center coordinates lie in a compact
set, and the constraints are closed.

This paper proves exact global optimality, rather than only a lower bound for
a selected subfamily or a local statement near a proposed configuration.  The
proof is a composition of geometric inequalities with finite certificates.
Its central idea is to turn each possible placement into one of finitely many
radial boxes and cyclic-sector assignments.  Most assignments close directly
by rational radial or angular-cycle bounds.  The remaining cases split into
two geometric families:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a seven-circle core in one fixed cyclic order, ruled out by two
        angular sums whose monotonicities point in opposite directions as
        the radius of circle $10$ varies;
  \item four exceptional assignments, projected to a nine-circle order and
        ruled out by a finite negative-cycle certificate.
\end{enumerate}
The angle barrier applies to every residual main-order case by a local
radius estimate from the non-overlap and containment constraints.  Finally,
the same two angular equations that force the lower bound also define a
configuration at $\Rcrit$.

We first define and realize the critical value, then reduce every possible
packing below the tree cap to a finite radial and angular case list.  The
seven-circle barrier and the two exceptional-order certificates close the
remaining cases.  Section~\ref{sec:verification} describes the exact
replay and its trust boundary.

Computer-assisted circle-packing research includes several related but
distinct settings.  Mark\'ot gives interval-based optimality proofs for 31,
32, and 33 equal circles in a square \cite{Markot2021}.  For unequal circles
in circular containers, Huang et al. develop packing heuristics
\cite{Huang2006}, while L\'opez and Beasley study a fixed-container
formulation-space search heuristic \cite{LopezBeasley2016}.  Fekete,
Keldenich, and Scheffer prove a tight universal worst-case density bound of
$1/2$ for arbitrary disk sets \cite{Fekete2023}.  These works address
equal-circle square packing, heuristic optimization, or universal density
guarantees; here we determine the exact minimum radius for one prescribed
ten-circle instance using a finite global certificate.

\section{Problem and exact statement}

\subsection{Feasibility and the candidate value}

For $\rho>0$, call $\rho$ feasible if there are centers satisfying
\eqref{eq:containment}--\eqref{eq:separation} with $R=\rho$.  Feasibility is
monotone in $\rho$: a configuration feasible at $\rho$ is feasible at every
larger container radius.  Thus a certified infeasibility result at one radius
is a lower bound for $\Ropt$.

We define the exact candidate through the angular quantities introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:critical}.  Let
\[
\begin{aligned}
  J_t&=[1.0037160860750841,\;1.0037160860750846],\\
  J_R&=[8.3034681221114890,\;8.3034681221114900].
\end{aligned}
\]
The number $\Rcrit$ is the $R$-coordinate of the unique pair
$(t_{\mathrm{crit}},R_{\mathrm{crit}})\in J_t\times J_R$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
  A(t_{\mathrm{crit}},R_{\mathrm{crit}})=2\pi,
  \qquad
  B(t_{\mathrm{crit}},R_{\mathrm{crit}})=2\pi.
  \label{eq:root-definition}
\end{equation}
The existence and uniqueness of this pair, as well as the strict upper
endpoint inequality, are proved by exact rational interval checks in
Section~\ref{sec:barrier}.  In particular,
\begin{equation}
  8.303468122111489\le \Rcrit
  < 8.303468122111490 = \Rcap.
  \label{eq:root-bracket}
\end{equation}
The equations, rather than a rounded decimal, define the exact value in this
paper.

\begin{theorem}[Global optimality]
\label{thm:global}
For ten circles of radii $1,\sqrt2,\ldots,\sqrt{10}$ in a circular
container,
\[
  \boxed{\Ropt=\Rcrit.}
\]
More precisely, no packing exists for any $R<\Rcrit$, and a packing exists
at $R=\Rcrit$.
\end{theorem}
The theorem determines the optimal radius; it does not assert uniqueness of the
packing that attains it.

The exhaustive radial case tree covers every configuration with
$R\le\Rcap$.  Its residual cases are closed by the angle barrier and the
nine-circle negative-cycle certificate.  Since $\Rcrit<\Rcap$, these facts
cover every putative packing below $\Rcrit$.  The matching construction is
given in Section~\ref{sec:witness}.

\subsection{Exact specification and scope}

The value in Theorem~\ref{thm:global} is the exact root-defined real number,
not the rounded decimal in \eqref{eq:root-bracket}; uniqueness refers to the
defining root pair, not to the packing attaining the optimum.

\section{Critical configuration and exact upper bound}
\label{sec:critical}

\subsection{The core order and two angular closure equations}

Consider the seven-circle core
\[
  K=\{2,5,6,7,8,9,10\},\qquad
  W=K\setminus\{10\}=\{2,5,6,7,8,9\}.
\]
For a center $p_i$, put $s_i=\norm{p_i}$.  For any two circles with
$r_i+r_j\le s_i+s_j$ and $|s_i-s_j|<r_i+r_j$, define their necessary
smaller central angle by
\begin{equation}
  \phi_{ij}(s_i,s_j)=
  \arccos\!\left(
    \frac{s_i^2+s_j^2-(r_i+r_j)^2}{2s_is_j}
  \right).
  \label{eq:phi}
\end{equation}
The law of cosines and non-overlap imply that the directed angular gap from
one center direction to another is at least this smaller angle.  In a
cyclic order, all directed gaps sum to $2\pi$.

The candidate core order is
\[
  (10,5,7,9,2,8,6).
\]
For $i\in W$ put
\[
 a_i(R)=R-r_i,\qquad D_i=r_{10}+r_i.
\]
When the six circles in $W$ are at the container wall, the angle between
circle $10$ at radial coordinate $t=s_{10}$ and circle $i$ is
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_i(t,R)=
  \arccos\!\left(
    \frac{t^2+a_i(R)^2-D_i^2}{2t\,a_i(R)}
  \right).
  \label{eq:alpha}
\end{equation}
For two wall circles $i,j\in W$, their necessary angle is
\begin{equation}
  \beta_{ij}(R)=
  2\arcsin\sqrt{\frac{r_ir_j}{a_i(R)a_j(R)}}.
  \label{eq:beta}
\end{equation}
Define
\begin{align}
 C(R)&=\beta_{79}(R)+\beta_{92}(R)+\beta_{28}(R)+\beta_{86}(R),\\
 A(t,R)&=\alpha_5(t,R)+\beta_{57}(R)+C(R)+\alpha_6(t,R),\\
 B(t,R)&=\alpha_7(t,R)+C(R)+\alpha_6(t,R).
 \label{eq:AB}
\end{align}

For the stated order, the directed loop
$10\to5\to7\to9\to2\to8\to6\to10$ has total angle $2\pi$ and therefore
requires $A_{\mathrm{radial}}\le2\pi$.  A second loop
$10\to7\to9\to2\to8\to6\to10$ skips circle $5$ in the angle count, but
circle $5$ remains present in the packing.  It gives the independent
necessary inequality $B_{\mathrm{radial}}\le2\pi$.  These two inequalities
are the key: their dependence on $t$ has opposite signs near the critical
pair.

\subsection{The exact critical pair}

The following derivative formulas explain how the candidate is isolated.
For $i\in\{5,6,7\}$ let
\[
 b_i=a_i(R)^2-D_i^2,\qquad
 H_i=4a_i(R)^2t^2-(t^2+b_i)^2.
\]
Where the angles are in $(0,\pi)$,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\partial\alpha_i}{\partial t}
   =\frac{b_i-t^2}{t\sqrt{H_i}},\qquad
 \frac{\partial\alpha_i}{\partial R}
   =-\frac{a_i(R)^2+D_i^2-t^2}{a_i(R)\sqrt{H_i}}.
 \label{eq:alpha-derivatives}
\end{equation}
For a wall-wall angle,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\partial\beta_{ij}}{\partial R}
  =-\left(\frac1{a_i(R)}+\frac1{a_j(R)}\right)
    \sqrt{\frac{r_ir_j}{a_i(R)a_j(R)-r_ir_j}}.
 \label{eq:beta-derivative}
\end{equation}
Exact sign checks establish $A_t>0$ and $B_t<0$ throughout $t\in I$ when
$R\in J_R$.  Separate interval checks establish $A_R<0$ and $B_R<0$ for
$t\in I$ and $\frac{15947}{2000}\le R\le8.30346812212$:
\begin{equation}
  A_t>0,\qquad B_t<0,\qquad A_R<0,\qquad B_R<0.
  \label{eq:root-signs}
\end{equation}
For example, $A_t>0$ follows from $(\alpha_5)_t>0$ and
$(\alpha_6)_t>0$.  To see the less immediate sign $B_t<0$, put
$z=t^2$, $b_i=a_i^2-D_i^2$, and
$H_i=4a_i^2z-(z+b_i)^2$.  Then
\[
 B_t=-\frac{z-b_7}{t\sqrt{H_7}}
       +\frac{b_6-z}{t\sqrt{H_6}}.
\]
Here $z-b_7>0$ and $b_6-z>0$ on the certified rectangle.  Squaring the
positive terms shows that $B_t<0$ follows from
\begin{align*}
 (z-b_7)^2H_6-(b_6-z)^2H_7&=4zQ(z),\\
 Q(z)&=(D_6^2-D_7^2)z^2
       +2(a_7^2b_6-a_6^2b_7)z\\
     &\quad+a_6^2b_7^2-a_7^2b_6^2+(b_6-b_7)b_6b_7.
\end{align*}
The identity follows by expansion, and the leading coefficient is negative,
so $Q$ is concave.  On
$z\in[0.73753744,1.26900225]$, exact interval evaluation gives endpoint
lower bounds greater than $50.40$ and $205.27$.  Concavity puts $Q$ above the
chord joining these positive endpoint values, proving $Q>0$ throughout.

To prove existence, write
\[
 F(t,R)=A(t,R)-B(t,R)
       =\alpha_5(t,R)+\beta_{57}(R)-\alpha_7(t,R),
 \qquad
 G(t,R)=B(t,R)-2\pi.
\]
The exact checker proves opposite signs for $F$ on the lower and upper
$t$-edges of the rational rectangle, and opposite signs for $G$ on the lower
and upper $R$-edges.  Table~\ref{tab:root-edge-signs} gives outward-rounded
rational enclosures for the full edge ranges; these include variation along
each edge rather than sampling only endpoints.  Since $F_t>0$,
for every $R$ in the rectangle there is exactly one $t(R)$ with $F=0$.
Continuity and the edge signs for $G$ then give an $R$ with $G=0$ by the
intermediate value theorem.  This is precisely a solution of
$A=B=2\pi$.

The solution is unique.  At fixed $R$, $A_t>0$ gives at most one solution
to $A=2\pi$.  If two simultaneous solutions had $R_1<R_2$, then
$A(t_1,R_2)<2\pi$ and $B(t_1,R_2)<2\pi$, because both decrease with $R$.
Restoring $A=2\pi$ at $R_2$ would require increasing $t$, while restoring
$B=2\pi$ would require decreasing $t$, since $A_t>0$ and $B_t<0$.  Both
cannot hold at the same $t$.  Thus the pair is unique.  The exact endpoint
checks further show
\[
  t_{\mathrm{crit}}\in J_t,\qquad
  8.303468122111489\le \Rcrit<8.303468122111490.
\]

All angle and derivative signs just used are checked without floating-point
decisions.  The verifier evaluates rational enclosures for square roots,
uses Machin's identity with alternating arctangent series to enclose $\pi$,
and uses half-angle arctangent expansions with rational remainder bounds for
the inverse trigonometric terms.

\subsection{An exact ten-circle witness}
\label{sec:witness}

At $(t_{\mathrm{crit}},\Rcrit)$, put circle $10$ at radial distance
$t_{\mathrm{crit}}$ and put each $i\in W$ at its wall radius
$a_i(\Rcrit)=\Rcrit-r_i$.  Use the following polar angles:
\begin{align*}
 \theta_{10}&=0,\\
 \theta_5&=\alpha_5,\\
 \theta_7&=\alpha_5+\beta_{57}=\alpha_7,\\
 \theta_9&=\theta_7+\beta_{79},\\
 \theta_2&=\theta_9+\beta_{92},\\
 \theta_8&=\theta_2+\beta_{28},\\
 \theta_6&=\theta_8+\beta_{86}=2\pi-\alpha_6.
\end{align*}
The identities $\alpha_5+\beta_{57}=\alpha_7$ and
$\alpha_7+C+\alpha_6=2\pi$ are exactly the two root equations.  They make
the eight pairs
\[
 (10,5),(10,7),(10,6),(5,7),(7,9),(9,2),(2,8),(8,6)
\]
tangent.  The remaining 13 pairs within the seven-circle core are
strictly separated throughout the certified root rectangle.

Place the three other centers at the following fixed rational coordinates:
\[
 p_1=(-3.9798,\;6.1148),\qquad
 p_3=(6.4777,\;0.8849),\qquad
 p_4=(5.5222,\;-2.7574).
\]
(The terminating decimals here denote exact rationals.)  The witness replay
checks all 37 non-contact pairs among the ten circles and verifies strict
container inclusion for circles $1,3,4$ and circle $10$, uniformly over the
rational enclosure of $(t_{\mathrm{crit}},\Rcrit)$.  The six circles in $W$
are on the wall, and all remaining inequalities have positive certified
slack.  Hence this is an actual feasible configuration at the exact root,
not merely a rounded numerical picture.

\section{Global reduction to finite cases}
\label{sec:overview}

A packing has ten radial coordinates $s_i=\norm{p_i}$ and ten center
directions.  The proof infrastructure first subdivides the allowed radial
domain by rational midpoint splits.  On each radial box, exact necessary
conditions determine which cyclic orders and angular-sector assignments
remain possible.  These assignments form the finite ledger called the
global radial tree.

At a terminal case, one of the following happens:
\begin{itemize}
  \item interval-safe radial inequalities contradict containment or
        non-overlap;
  \item lower bounds on pair angles contradict the $2\pi$ angular budget,
        often through a Farkas multiplier certificate;
  \item a difference-constraint graph contains a strictly negative directed
        cycle;
  \item the assignment has the main seven-circle order and radial range, so
        the two-sum angle barrier applies;
  \item the assignment is one of the four alternative masks, so its
        nine-circle projection is covered by the negative-cycle tree.
\end{itemize}
Every split is rational and its children cover the parent box exactly.  Thus
the finite tree is a cover argument, not a sampling of candidate
configurations.

The global cap is
\[
  \Rcap=8.303468122111490.
\]
Because the exact root lies strictly below this cap, it is enough for the
tree to cover all configurations up to $\Rcap$; no comparison of floating
approximations is used to bridge a gap.  This tree is a coverage certificate,
not a claim that every radius $R\le\Rcap$ is infeasible.  The composed
exclusion applies to hypothetical packings with $R<\Rcrit$; the exact witness
in Section~\ref{sec:witness} establishes feasibility at $R=\Rcrit$.

\subsection{The four largest circles force a four-sector skeleton}
\label{sec:large-skeleton}

The circles with labels $7,8,9,10$ provide the global angular scaffold.
For every feasible packing with $R\le U$, containment and pairwise
non-overlap imply
\begin{equation}
  s_i\le U-r_i,\qquad
  s_i\ge r_i+2r_j-U\quad(i\ne j).
  \label{eq:large-radial-bounds}
\end{equation}
The first inequality is containment.  For the second, use
$r_i+r_j\le\norm{p_i-p_j}\le s_i+s_j$ and $s_j\le U-r_j$.
In particular, the directions of circles $7,8,9,10$ are defined: the exact
rational enclosures used by the certificate give the safe lower bounds
$s_7>0.6668$, $s_8>0.8495$, $s_9>1.0210$, and $s_{10}>0.8588$.

\begin{lemma}[The four largest centers are not contained in a semicircle]
\label{lem:large-four-semicircle}
For every packing with $R\le U$, the four center directions of circles
$7,8,9,10$ are not all contained in any closed semicircle.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $i,j\in\{7,8,9,10\}$, the law of cosines and
\eqref{eq:large-radial-bounds} give a lower bound $q_{ij}$ on the smaller
central angle between $p_i$ and $p_j$.  The six accepted rational angle
ticks are listed in Table~\ref{tab:large-four-angles}; their exact
acceptance check uses the four-corner lemma below and a rational cosine
Taylor lower bound.  The verifier expands all $4!=24$ possible linear orders
of these directions along a putative semicircle.  In every order, the sum of
the three consecutive pair bounds exceeds
$33019/10000=3.3019$, whereas the rational upper bound
$\pi_+=3927/1250=3.1416$ satisfies $\pi<\pi_+$.  The strict margin is
$1603/10000$.  Four directions lying in a closed semicircle would have a
linear order whose three consecutive angular gaps sum to at most $\pi$,
contradicting these lower bounds.
\end{proof}

Consequently, when the four large directions are read counterclockwise,
each of the four gaps between consecutive directions is strictly less than
$\pi$: a gap at least $\pi$ would put the other three directions in a
closed semicircle.  These four rays split the disk into four sectors.  Assign
each of circles $1,\ldots,6$ to the sector containing its center direction;
a direction exactly on a boundary may be assigned to either neighboring
sector.  If a small-circle center is the origin, assign it arbitrarily:
the corresponding radial interval includes zero, every incident angular
lower bound is set to zero, and the checker deletes that label only when
testing projected orders.  Thus this convention does not exclude a
configuration.

Rooting the cyclic order at circle $10$ and identifying mirror images leaves

\subsection{Radial boxes cover the full candidate domain}
\label{sec:radial-tree}

Let $\underline r_i$ be the rational lower endpoint for $\sqrt{i}$ stored
in the certificate.  The initial radial box is
\begin{equation}
  s_i\in[\ell_i,u_i],\qquad
  \ell_i=\max\!\left(0,\max_{j\ne i}
       (\underline r_i+2\underline r_j-U)\right),\quad
  u_i=U-\underline r_i.
  \label{eq:initial-radial-box}
\end{equation}
It contains every feasible radial vector with $R\le U$.  The certificate
then recursively bisects one coordinate at a rational cut.  The two closed
children share the cut and cover the parent interval, so induction over the
tree shows that its leaves cover the entire initial box; they are not samples
of candidate placements.  The replay checks all $2{,}401$ nodes, all $1{,}200$
splits, and all $1{,}201$ leaves, including reachability and absence of
duplicate nodes.

At a box with upper endpoints $u_i$, if
$u_i+u_j<\underline r_i+\underline r_j$ for some pair, the box is empty by
non-overlap.  Otherwise the checker propagates the pair-sum inequalities to
raise lower endpoints.  This propagation is an outward-safe contraction:
it removes only radial values that cannot satisfy
$s_i+s_j\ge r_i+r_j$.

\subsection{Exact angular relaxations and box-closing rules}
\label{sec:box-rules}

For a pair with required center distance $d_{ij}=r_i+r_j$, define
\[
  c_{ij}(x,y)=\frac{x^2+y^2-d_{ij}^2}{2xy}.
\]
The necessary smaller central angle is $\arccos(c_{ij})$.  On a positive
radial rectangle, $c_{ij}$ attains its maximum at a corner:

\begin{lemma}[Four-corner bound for the radial cosine]
\label{lem:corner-bound}
Let $d>0$ and let $x,y$ range over a positive rectangle.  The maximum of
\[
  c(x,y)=\frac{x^2+y^2-d^2}{2xy}
\]
on that rectangle is attained at one of its four corners.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For fixed $y>0$,
\[
  \frac{\partial c}{\partial x}
   =\frac{x^2-y^2+d^2}{2yx^2}.
\]
Its numerator is strictly increasing in $x$ and has at most one zero; if a
zero occurs, the derivative changes from negative to positive, so the
stationary point is a minimum.  The maximum over the $x$-interval is
therefore at an endpoint.  Applying the same argument in $y$ to each of
those two edges proves the corner claim.
\end{proof}

The certificate chooses a rational angle tick $q_{ij}\ge0$ for each pair.
It is accepted only if a rational Taylor lower bound for $\cos(q_{ij})$
dominates the four-corner upper bound for $c_{ij}$.  Since cosine decreases
on $[0,\pi]$, every feasible smaller central angle is then at least
$q_{ij}$.  If a radial interval allows a center at the origin, its incident
ticks are set to zero rather than assigning a direction that may not exist.

For a proposed cyclic order, lift the center angles to an increasing sequence
$\theta_0<\cdots<\theta_{n-1}<\theta_0+2\pi$.  If the label at position $a$
precedes the label at position $b$, the necessary pair-angle bounds give
\[
  q_{ab}\le\theta_b-\theta_a\le2\pi_+-q_{ab}.
\]
The four large-circle skeleton adds the fact that its four consecutive gaps
are below $\pi$.  These are difference constraints of the form
$\theta_v-\theta_u\le w(u,v)$.

\begin{lemma}[Negative-cycle criterion for angular constraints]
\label{lem:negative-cycle}
If a finite system of difference constraints
$\theta_v-\theta_u\le w(u,v)$ contains a directed cycle whose edge weights
sum to a strictly negative number, the system has no solution.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Summing the inequalities around the cycle makes the left side telescope to
zero, so feasibility would imply $0\le\sum w(u,v)<0$.  The checker also
verifies that each recorded cycle is one simple directed cycle and recomputes
its sum with exact rational arithmetic.
\end{proof}

Some boxes yield a feasible potential vector for these relaxed angular
constraints.  The verifier labels such a vector \texttt{ANGLE\_MODEL}; it is
only a model of necessary inequalities, not a set of Cartesian centers and
not a packing witness.  Such a box remains open for the later geometric
theorems.

In a smaller number of cases, the angular constraints imply a rational upper
bound $\Gamma_{ij}<\pi$ on the smaller angle between a pair.  If
$c^-\le\cos(\Gamma_{ij})$ is the rational Taylor lower bound, then the
actual smaller angle $\gamma_{ij}$ satisfies
$\cos(\gamma_{ij})\ge c^-$.  Throughout the radial box this gives
\[
  \norm{p_i-p_j}^2
  \le \max_{x\in I_i,y\in I_j}
       (x^2+y^2-2xyc^-).
\]
The quadratic is convex on the rectangle because $-1\le c^-\le1$, so its
maximum is attained at a corner.  If its verified upper bound is below
$(r_i+r_j)^2$, the circles must overlap, closing the box by a
\texttt{PAIR\_DISTANCE} witness.  Thus the original tree uses radial
pair-sum contradictions, angular negative cycles, and these angle-to-distance
contradictions.  The retained \texttt{ANGLE\_MODEL} cases and the
147 terminal records formerly closed by a local rule are checked again by
the global geometric barriers; the local conclusion is not used as a final
case closure.

\section{The seven-circle angular barrier}
\label{sec:barrier}

\subsection{Why the radial coordinates can be replaced by wall values}

The main order cases do not assume that the circles $i\in W$ are at the wall.
This is important: the theorem does not move disks, and it does not assume
the older sufficient conditions for physically pushing disks outward.

For $d_{ij}=r_i+r_j$, differentiation of \eqref{eq:phi} gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\partial\phi_{ij}}{\partial s_i}
  =-\frac{s_i^2-s_j^2+d_{ij}^2}
          {2s_i^2s_j\sin\phi_{ij}}.
 \label{eq:phi-derivative}
\end{equation}
The case boxes imply, for $t=s_{10}\in I=[0.8588,1.1265]$ and
$\frac{15947}{2000}\le R\le\Rcap$,
\[
  r_{10}+r_i-t\le s_i\le \Rcap-r_i
       \qquad(i\in W).
 \label{eq:main-radial-box}
\]
The left inequality follows from
$r_{10}+r_i\le\norm{p_{10}-p_i}\le s_{10}+s_i$; the right one is
containment.  The actual verifier rounds the square-root endpoints outward
to rational bounds before doing its checks.

For most coordinates, \eqref{eq:phi-derivative} has a directly certified
negative sign in each angular sum.  Three shared coordinates require a
combined derivative check because their two adjacent pair contributions
can have opposite signs.  For $(i,g,b)=(9,7,2),(8,6,2),(2,9,8)$, exact
rational bounds are applied to the combined numerator
\[
 \Lambda_i=\frac{X_{ig}}{s_g\sin\phi_{ig}}
          +\frac{X_{ib}}{s_b\sin\phi_{ib}},
 \qquad X_{ij}=s_i^2-s_j^2+(r_i+r_j)^2.
\]
The derivative of the two adjacent angles with respect to $s_i$ is
$-\Lambda_i/(2s_i^2)$.  Exact lower bounds for $\Lambda_i$ are:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cc}
\toprule
coordinate $s_i$ & certified lower bound for $\Lambda_i$\\
\midrule
$s_9$ & $>3.193809$\\
$s_8$ & $>0.143756$\\
$s_2$ & $>0.589640$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The smaller angular sines in the two potentially adverse pairs $(9,2)$ and
$(2,8)$ are bounded below by $3/5$; the remaining terms have positive
rational lower bounds.  For the individual terms, the checker certifies
$X_{ij}>0$ on the same radial rectangles.  It also verifies
$s_i+s_j>r_i+r_j$ and $|s_i-s_j|<r_i+r_j$ for every angle used, throughout
each interpolation from the original $s_i$ to $R-r_i$.  Thus every angle
remains real and continuous during this analytic substitution, and both full
radial sums are non-increasing in every $s_i$.

Starting from any feasible packing in the main order, replace its six radial
coordinates $s_i$ one at a time by $R-r_i$ only inside the angle formulas.
This is an analytic comparison, not a physical movement.  Since each angular
sum is non-increasing as any $s_i$ increases, the wall-value sums satisfy
\[
  A(t,R)\le A_{\mathrm{radial}}\le2\pi,\qquad
  B(t,R)\le B_{\mathrm{radial}}\le2\pi.
 \label{eq:wall-angle-necessary}
\]
This is the wall-free angular barrier.

\begin{lemma}[A radius floor for the main core]
\label{lem:main-radius-floor}
If a feasible main-core packing has $t=s_{10}\le1.1265$, then
\[
 R>\frac{15947}{2000}=7.9735.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Non-overlap of circles $9$ and $10$ gives
$s_9+t\ge r_9+r_{10}=3+\sqrt{10}$.  Containment of circle $9$ gives
$R\ge3+s_9$.  Since $\sqrt{10}>3.1$ and $t\le1.1265$,
\[
 R\ge6+\sqrt{10}-t
   \ge6+\sqrt{10}-1.1265
   >6+3.1-1.1265
   =7.9735.
\]
\end{proof}

\subsection{Excluding every radius below the critical value}

Suppose a main-order packing occurs in a tree box.  Its disk-$10$ coordinate
lies in $I$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:main-radius-floor} puts its radius in the
certified derivative range.  If $R<\Rcrit$, then by
\eqref{eq:wall-angle-necessary}, it must satisfy $A(t,R)\le2\pi$ and
$B(t,R)\le2\pi$.

If $t\ge t_{\mathrm{crit}}$, then $A_R<0$ on the interval from $R$ to
$R_{\mathrm{crit}}$, while $A_t>0$ at $R_{\mathrm{crit}}$, so
\[
 A(t,R)>A(t,\Rcrit)
          \ge A(t_{\mathrm{crit}},\Rcrit)=2\pi,
\]
a contradiction.  If $t\le t_{\mathrm{crit}}$, then $B_t<0$ and
$B_R<0$ imply
\[
 B(t,R)>B(t,\Rcrit)
          \ge B(t_{\mathrm{crit}},\Rcrit)=2\pi,
\]
also a contradiction.  These two cases cover every value of $t$.  Thus any
main-order case satisfying the tree's radial hypotheses is impossible below
$\Rcrit$.

\section{The four exceptional cases: a negative-cycle proof}
\label{sec:four-cases}

The four remaining assignments have a different large-circle skeleton and
are not covered by the preceding seven-circle theorem.  Their masks are
$(5,16,40,2)$ (three assignments) and $(4,16,41,2)$ (one assignment).
After projecting away circle $1$, their nine-circle order is
\[
 P=(10,3,7,5,8,4,6,9,2).
\]
The only ambiguity in the relative placement of circles $4$ and $6$ gives
the second possible order
\[
 Q=(10,3,7,5,8,6,4,9,2).
\]
The exact certificate covers both orders; the four assignments in the global
tree project to $P$.

\subsection{From radial boxes to angle-difference constraints}

Fix either order.  For a radial box $s_i\in[\ell_i,u_i]$, containment gives
$0\le s_i\le\Rcap_+-r_i$, where
\[
  \Rcap_+=8.30346812212>\Rcap.
\]
Non-overlap also requires $s_i+s_j\ge r_i+r_j$.  This inequality safely
tightens the lower endpoints of each box.  If any lower endpoint is zero,
the angle lower bound involving that coordinate is set to zero, which is
conservative.

For each pair, a nonnegative rational angle tick $q_{ij}$ is stored.  It is
accepted only after an exact rational Taylor inequality certifies
$\cos(q_{ij})>c_{ij}$ throughout the box.  Floating-point inverse cosine is
used only when searching for candidate ticks; it has no role in replay.
Consequently any feasible angular difference $\Delta_{ij}$ in the fixed
cyclic order must satisfy
\[
  q_{ij}\le \Delta_{ij}\le 2\pi_+-q_{ij},
\]
where $\pi_+$ is a rational upper bound for $\pi$ obtained from Machin's
identity and alternating arctangent series.

The four-corner bound and negative-cycle criterion from
Section~\ref{sec:box-rules} apply to these ticks.  The certificate replays
every listed cycle with its exact edge weights; the following table
summarizes the two trees.

\section{Composition of the global finite proof}
\label{sec:composition}

The proof uses a complete rational-split tree for the radial domain up to
$\Rcap$.  This tree covers the candidate radial domain; it does not by itself
claim infeasibility at every radius below $\Rcap$.  Its replay checks
\[
  2{,}401\ \text{nodes},\qquad
  1{,}200\ \text{splits},\qquad
  1{,}201\ \text{leaves}.
\]
\subsection{Sector masks and complete case accounting}

For each radial leaf, the checker considers the three canonical cyclic
orders of the four-circle skeleton $\sigma=(\sigma_0,\sigma_1,\sigma_2,
\sigma_3)$, rooted at circle $10$ and identified up to reflection.  The six
remaining circles have labels $1,\ldots,6$.  Each is assigned to one of the
four sectors after $\sigma_k$ and before $\sigma_{k+1}$ (indices modulo four).
If $S_k$ is the set assigned to sector $k$, its integer mask is
\begin{equation}
  M_k=\sum_{i\in S_k}2^{i-1},\qquad k=0,1,2,3.
  \label{eq:sector-mask}
\end{equation}
The tuple $(M_0,M_1,M_2,M_3)$ records the assignment: every label occurs in
exactly one sector, and each of the six labels has four choices.  Thus there
are $4^6$ masks for each skeleton and $3\cdot4^6=12{,}288$ sector
assignments per radial leaf.  With $1{,}201$ leaves, the total is
\[
  1{,}201\cdot3\cdot4^6=14{,}757{,}888.
\]
For example, the mask tuple $(5,16,40,2)$ assigns label sets
$(\{1,3\},\{5\},\{4,6\},\{2\})$ to the four successive sectors.
When all angular lower bounds incident to a disk vanish, the replay may
clear that label's bit in an effective mask; this merges equivalent order
checks but does not remove any of the $3\cdot4^6$ original sector assignments
from the coverage count.

Under the tree's original rules, $14{,}757{,}691$ assignments were closed
and $197$ remained unresolved.  The closed count includes local-terminal
cases.  The $147$ local records described below are rechecked because their
earlier closure used a local premise.  The residual assignments divide as
follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The $193$ main cases have seven-circle core order
        $(10,5,\allowbreak 7,9,\allowbreak 2,8,6)$ and meet the wall-free
        barrier hypotheses;
  \item Four exceptional masks have nine-circle projection $P$ after
        deleting circle $1$.  Both $P$ and $Q$ are covered by the
        negative-cycle certificate.
\end{itemize}
The $193$ residual main-order cases have disk-$10$ radial intervals
contained in $[0.8588,1.1265]$, which places them in the range required by
the seven-circle barrier.  Both exceptional orders $P$ and $Q$ have seven-circle core
projection $(10,7,5,8,6,9,2)$, so they are not passed to the main-order
barrier.
The masks are $(5,16,40,2)$ in three cases and $(4,16,41,2)$ in one.
The composition verifier also reclassifies $145$ core-order terminal models
and $2$ full-order terminal witnesses through the same seven-circle angle
barrier.  This removes their dependence on the previous local terminal rule.
Each of these $147$ records has the main core order and the required
disk-$10$ interval, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:main-radius-floor} supplies the radius range required by the
barrier.  These are reclassifications of terminal records already included
in the tree's prior count, not additional sector assignments.  For every
hypothetical packing with $R<\Rcrit$, the composed rules rule out all
$14{,}757{,}888$ assignments.  This statement does not exclude the feasible
boundary case $R=\Rcrit$.

\begin{lemma}[Completeness of sector-order projection]
\label{lem:sector-projection}
Fix a four-circle skeleton $\sigma$ in cyclic order and effective sector
masks $M=(M_0,M_1,M_2,M_3)$ for the remaining circles with positive angular
lower bounds.  The set $\Pi(\sigma,M)$ formed by preserving the skeleton
order and taking every permutation of the assigned labels within each sector
contains every order compatible with that assignment.  Every full order,
after deleting labels whose angular lower bounds vanish, restricts to one of
these projections.  Consequently, if every order in $\Pi(\sigma,M)$ has its
seven-circle restriction equal to
$(10,5,7,9,2,8,6)$, the main angle barrier applies to every packing in that
mask.  If every order instead has its nine-circle restriction, after
deleting circle $1$, in $\{P,Q\}$, the negative-cycle certificate applies
to every packing in that mask.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Read any compatible cyclic order starting at the first skeleton circle.
Each remaining circle with a positive angular lower bound lies in one sector
between consecutive skeleton circles.  Its within-sector order is one of the
permutations enumerated in $\Pi(\sigma,M)$; ties can be assigned either
consistent order and both are included.  Circles with zero lower bounds are
deleted, which only relaxes the necessary inequalities.  The composition
checker constructs the whole projection set for each residual mask and
verifies the required restrictions for every member; it does not infer them
from the single order stored with an angular model.
\end{proof}

For the $197$ residual sector assignments, the replay checks $790$ projected
orders in total.  All $772$ projections in the main-order groups restrict to
the stated seven-circle order.  All $18$ projections in the exceptional
groups restrict, after deleting circle $1$, to $P$ or $Q$.  The $147$ local
terminal models are separately replayed order by order through the main
barrier.  These finite checks make the connection from the case tree to the
two geometric theorems explicit.

\section{Reproducibility and trust boundary}
\label{sec:verification}

The complete composition is replayed from the repository root with
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
uv run --locked python \textbackslash\\
\hspace*{1em}\path{tools/verify_global_optimality_completion.py}
\end{quote}
This replay checks the radial tree, theorem links, exact root and witness,
and both exceptional orders.  The JSON report is written to
\texttt{artifacts/}.

All decisive arithmetic in these replayers is integer or rational arithmetic.
Square roots are enclosed with integer square-root bounds; the trigonometric
inequalities are reduced to rational Taylor bounds and rational enclosures of
$\pi$.  Floating-point routines may help generate candidate split points or
angle ticks, but the verifier rechecks the accepted claims using exact
inequalities.  This separates fast certificate discovery from the smaller
checking computation.

The trust boundary is explicit.  This is a computer-assisted proof whose
soundness depends on the correctness of the Python verifiers, the Python
runtime's integer and \texttt{Fraction} semantics, and the certificate inputs
whose hashes the composition checker records.  The proof is not formalized
end-to-end in Lean.  In particular, references to old Lean developments or
the MPFI generator do not substitute for the exact replay used here.

The composition entry point rejects Python's optimized modes, because some
component checkers use \texttt{assert} statements for certificate conditions;
running it with \texttt{-O} or \texttt{-OO} would otherwise remove those
checks.  The SHA-256 manifest covers the exact case tree, theorem data,
verifiers, locked Python environment, composition note, root README, and the
manuscript source and PDF.  All of these are committed as one reproducible
source unit.

The composition also replays the previous local-terminal classifier to
identify the $145$ core-order models and two full-order records that had been
closed by that rule.  It does not accept those local conclusions as final
closures: each selected record is separately checked against the seven-circle
angle barrier and its required radial interval.  The local verifier therefore
remains in the case-selection and replay dependency chain, while the final
geometric implication for those records is the global barrier.

The repository's global-optimality completion note and the
\path{proof/global_completion/} directory contain the individual theorem
proofs and certificate details.  The composition report records SHA-256
hashes for these inputs so a replay can be tied to the checked inputs.

\section{Conclusion}

The finite tree and its geometric terminal theorems give a global lower bound
at the exact angle-defined value $\Rcrit$.  The root construction supplies a
ten-circle packing at the same value.  Therefore the minimum container radius
is exactly $\Rcrit$, as claimed in Theorem~\ref{thm:global}.

The coupled-angle equations give an exact specification of the optimum; the
finite decimals serve only as certified enclosures for the root and as
convenient data for replay.
